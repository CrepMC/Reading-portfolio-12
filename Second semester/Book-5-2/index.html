Why there's no place like home for the holidays

Frank T. McAndrew   /   2017   /   Psychology explainer

Is home an address, a memory, or a relationship?

01

What I read

McAndrew explores why home can carry meaning far beyond a building. Emotional attachment to places helps organize identity, memories and relationships. Survey responses show that people may locate home where they grew up, where they live, where they spent the longest time, or where their family has roots. Cultural examples connect domestic spaces with order and community, while holiday rituals renew social bonds. The argument moves from familiar seasonal feelings to psychological and cultural explanations. Home emerges as a connection between past and present, rather than a location that everyone defines in the same way.

READING FOCUS

Compare meanings of home. The article connects place with identity, comfort, family roots, memories and rituals; a current address is only one possible meaning.

Evidence & analysis

CLAIM

Home is not a single location category.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDThe reported 2008 Pew responses distinguish birthplace, current residence, longest residence and family roots.

WHY IT MATTERSThe range of answers supports the idea that belonging cannot be inferred from an address alone.

CLAIM

Ritual makes attachment social.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDThe discussion of homecoming describes meals, gifts and visits renewing family connections.

WHY IT MATTERSHome is sustained through repeated relationships and practices, not just private affection for a building.

CLAIM

The structure expands a familiar feeling.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDSeasonal songs introduce a discussion of psychological language and cultural examples.

WHY IT MATTERSThis movement helps readers connect ordinary longing with a broader argument about identity and place.

05

My response

I think a person can have more than one home. McAndrew’s account of the Pew survey shows that some people think first of where they grew up, while others choose where they live now or where their family has roots. Those answers suggest different kinds of attachment. A person might turn to a childhood place for a sense of continuity and to a newer community for daily connection. Which place comes to mind can depend on what the person is looking for.
