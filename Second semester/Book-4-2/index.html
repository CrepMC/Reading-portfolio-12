Excerpt from "Roughing It": Lost in the Snow

Mark Twain   /   1872   /   Humorous travel narrative · excerpt

When does confidence become a dangerous substitute for evidence?

CONTENTS01What I read02Evidence & analysis03Explore the idea04Words to keep05My response06Source & connections
01

What I read

Twain’s travelers enter heavy snow trusting Ollendorff’s supposed instinct for direction. Fresh tracks seem to confirm their progress until they discover that they have been following their own circular route. They later follow a stagecoach, but its tracks disappear and rows of buried shrubs imitate roads. Their attempt to make a fire fails spectacularly, and the horses wander away. Exaggeration, ironic reversals and misplaced confidence create humor, while cold, darkness and the disappearance of another traveler keep real danger present. The excerpt repeatedly exposes the gap between what the group believes and what its observations actually establish.

READING FOCUS

Track obstacles in sequence: hidden roads, poor visibility, a circular route, disappearing tracks, misleading shrubs, cold, a failed fire and lost horses.

02

Evidence & analysis

CLAIM

The travelers mistake repetition for progress.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDIncreasingly fresh tracks are interpreted as evidence that another party is nearby.

WHY IT MATTERSThe later recognition that the tracks are their own turns earlier confidence into dramatic irony.

CLAIM

Humor does not erase danger.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDThe comic argument about direction sits beside the narrator’s account of the Swede’s disappearance.

WHY IT MATTERSThe narrator’s inferred fate for the Swede darkens the mood and raises the stakes; it is not an eyewitness report of his death.

CLAIM

Book knowledge is tested against experience.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDThe group trusts a method of making fire it has only read about, then scatters the twigs.

WHY IT MATTERSThe exaggerated failure satirizes unquestioned confidence, including the narrator’s own mistakes.

Reference key & reasoning

The fire fails and the horses are lost: 4 · Last. The final attempt follows their decision to stop and camp. Ollendorff promises reliable direction: 1 · First. The opening establishes confidence in his instinct. Stagecoach tracks disappear into misleading shrub rows: 3 · Third. This is the second loss of direction, after the circular route. 05

My response

The travelers read fresh tracks as evidence that someone else is ahead. That is believable at first, which makes the discovery that they have been following their own tracks funnier. Looking back, the fading landmarks and snow-covered road give them very little evidence about where they are. I like how Twain lets the same tracks support two stories. The difference comes from whether the travelers are willing to test what they assumed when they first saw them.
