Screen Addiction Among Teens: Is There Such A Thing?

Anya Kamenetz   /   2018   /   Explanatory reporting

Is time spent online enough to tell us whether technology is a problem?

CONTENTS01What I read02Evidence & analysis03Explore the idea04Words to keep05My response06Source & connections
01

What I read

Kamenetz investigates disagreement about the language of technology addiction while taking harmful patterns of use seriously. Experts differ over diagnosis and treatment, yet the article suggests that interference with relationships, school and everyday life matters more than hours alone. It considers counseling, habit changes and the difficulty of avoiding technology that students need. The focus then widens to companies whose advertising models reward keeping users engaged. By including both individual responses and changes to platform incentives, Kamenetz presents screen use as a complex relationship shaped by people and the systems around them.

READING FOCUS

Identify negative effects in context. The article describes interference with school and relationships, avoidance of problems, and compulsive patterns; it does not make screen duration alone a diagnosis.

02

Evidence & analysis

CLAIM

The article separates a contested label from observable harm.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASED“Dueling diagnoses” presents disagreement; “Measuring problematic use” asks whether media disrupts life.

WHY IT MATTERSThe contrast helps readers examine behavior without assuming all experts use the same diagnostic category.

CLAIM

Technology is difficult to avoid completely.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDIn “Seeking treatment,” students still need computers for school and social contact.

WHY IT MATTERSThe comparison with food emphasizes rebuilding a relationship rather than simply removing the object.

CLAIM

Responsibility extends to product design.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDIn “A public health approach,” Harris connects advertising income to attention-maximizing design.

WHY IT MATTERSThe shift from individual habits to company incentives broadens the possible solutions.

Explore the idea

Hypothetical situations

Look beyond the clock

TIME+CONTEXT+IMPACT
Perspective 01

A long study session

Scenario: a student spends several hours researching, then sleeps, meets friends and finishes other responsibilities.

What would the article ask?

Duration alone does not establish problematic use. Ask about purpose, control and interference with daily life. The scenario is hypothetical and cannot support a diagnosis.

Perspective 02

A disrupted routine

Scenario: a student repeatedly misses sleep and assignments to keep checking a feed, even when they intend to stop.

What would the article ask?

Repeated interference with sleep and school is more relevant to the article’s reasoning than a time total alone. Context and other possible difficulties would still matter; this is not a diagnostic tool.

Perspective 03

A demanding group chat

Scenario: a student values online friendships but feels pressured to answer during family meals.

What would the article ask?

Consider both the benefit of connection and the pressure of constant availability. The article supports examining habits and platform incentives together.

05

My response

Kamenetz’s discussion of how screen use can interfere with relationships made me think about who controls the conditions of screen use. Families can set a quiet time at night, and that might address interrupted sleep. If a platform makes money from keeping people scrolling, it has a reason to keep inviting them back. In that case, limits on interruptions and changes to defaults could help more people. I would judge their effect by looking at which everyday activities screen use begins to displace.
