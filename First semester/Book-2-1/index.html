READING 02 / 15   ·   Sports & wellbeing

The Pros and Cons of Youth Sports Aren't Only Physical

Marika Lindholm   /   2017   /   Commentary

What helps youth sports build confidence without making winning define a child’s worth?

CONTENTS01What I read02Evidence & analysis03Explore the idea04Words to keep05My response06Source & connections
01

What I read

Lindholm argues that the effects of youth sports reach into confidence, relationships and habits. Drawing on her teaching, research and parenting, she presents six psychological risks alongside six benefits. Pressure from adults, bullying coaches and an identity tied to winning can harm young athletes. Supportive coaching and practice can build resilience, collaboration, a work ethic and time-management skills. The same activity can strengthen self-esteem through improvement or undermine it when results determine a child’s sense of worth. Her advice asks parents to notice the conditions surrounding participation and reconsider an approach when enjoyment gives way to harm.

READING FOCUS

Track how psychological effects depend on the sporting environment. Risks include performance-based self-worth, bullying, unrealistic scholarship expectations, strained parent relationships, pressure and superiority. Benefits include handling feedback, efficacy, a work ethic, positive body image, resilience and self-management.

02

Evidence & analysis

CLAIM

Self-esteem depends on how success is understood.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDPsychological Risks, item 1, warns against tying identity to results. Psychological Benefits, item 2, describes confidence gained through practice and improvement.

WHY IT MATTERSPlacing these points together shows how judgments about performance can shape its emotional effect. Growth gives a child a different basis for confidence from constant demands to win.

CLAIM

Adults help create the conditions for learning.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDThe risks section criticizes humiliating coaches and over-invested parents. The benefits section describes learning to accept feedback and cooperate.

WHY IT MATTERSThe contrast gives parents something concrete to evaluate in a team’s culture. It also explains why correction delivered constructively can support learning.

CLAIM

The paired lists support a conditional argument.

EVIDENCE · PARAPHRASEDSix numbered risks are followed by six numbered benefits, then advice to reevaluate participation if it becomes harmful.

WHY IT MATTERSThe structure invites readers to weigh the conditions of participation. Direct advice and examples from teaching and parenting make the commentary practical, while its conclusion depends on how a child is doing.

03

Explore the idea

Benefits & risks

What shapes the psychological outcome?

Classify each example using Lindholm’s two lists of psychological effects. Consider the role of adults, feedback and expectations in each outcome.

Reference key & reasoning

Learning to use constructive feedback and work with teammates: Psychological benefit. Benefits, item 1, links accepting criticism and collaborating with skills useful in adulthood. Feeling that personal worth depends on winning: Psychological risk. Risks, item 1, warns that tying identity to performance can damage self-esteem after a loss. Organizing schoolwork around practice and competition: Psychological benefit. Benefits, item 6, describes self-regulation, organization and time management. Parents making affection depend on athletic results: Psychological risk. Risks, item 4, describes the strain created by over-invested parents. Developing confidence by improving a skill through practice: Psychological benefit. Benefits, item 2, connects effort and improvement with efficacy and self-esteem. Assuming sporting ability guarantees a college scholarship: Psychological risk. Risks, item 3, cautions against relying on that expectation and neglecting other interests.

Why does self-esteem appear on both sides?

Lindholm distinguishes confidence built through learning from self-worth made dependent on results. The conditions around participation influence its psychological effects. Her closing advice asks parents to pay attention to enjoyment and harm.

04

Words to keep

Open a word to reveal its meaning and an original example. Each word appears in the supplied reading.

demoralize

To weaken someone’s confidence or spirit.

Public humiliation can demoralize a player who is still learning.

efficacy

The ability to produce a desired result.

Improving a skill through practice can build a young athlete’s sense of efficacy.

resilience

The capacity to recover and adapt after setbacks.

Learning to face a defeat can help develop resilience.

self-regulation

Managing one’s behavior and choices to meet a goal.

Balancing practice with schoolwork requires self-regulation.

humility

Recognizing one’s limitations without assuming superiority over others.

Humility helps a successful player respect teammates and opponents.

adulation

Excessive admiration or praise.

Constant adulation can make it harder for an athlete to accept criticism.

05

My response

I would judge a youth team partly by how the adults respond after a loss. Lindholm connects growth with learning to use feedback, yet she also describes coaches who humiliate players and parents whose affection seems tied to results. A coach who explains a mistake and gives a player a chance to work on it makes improvement feel possible. I find the paired points about self-esteem especially convincing because they show how the meaning attached to a result can affect confidence. Enjoyment, effort and relationships would all matter in deciding whether a team is helping its players.
